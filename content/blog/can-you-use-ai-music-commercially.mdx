Yes, you can use AI-generated music commercially in 2026. But "can" comes with three layers that most YouTube tutorials skip: the tool tier you generate on, the label settlements that reshaped what platforms like [Suno](https://suno.ai) and [Udio](https://udio.com) let you export, and the copyright catch that decides whether anyone truly owns the track.

The starting point matters more than any prompt: **a purely AI-generated track is not copyrightable in the United States.** In March 2026 the Supreme Court declined to hear Thaler v. Perlmutter, leaving standing the rule that copyright requires human authorship. Your AI song can be licensed, monetized, and sold under a tool's commercial terms, but you cannot register it as your own copyrighted work unless humans contributed meaningfully: your lyrics, your vocals, your edits, your arrangement.

## The Copyright Catch: You Probably Do Not Own It

Two separate questions get bundled together, and untangling them answers most of the confusion. Licensing asks what the tool allows you to do with its output. Copyright asks whether anyone can own that output at all.

On licensing, the tools are explicit. [Suno](/reviews/suno)'s Pro plan at $10/month "removes the watermark and gives you commercial usage rights," which is standard across the category: pay, and you can monetize. [Mureka](https://mureka.ai) grants full commercial rights on Pro at $9/month, the cheapest entry point we found. [AIVA](/reviews/aiva) goes furthest, granting 100% copyright ownership on its Pro plan with zero royalty sharing, the only tool in the category making that promise.

On copyright, the law is the constraint. The US Copyright Office requires you to disclose AI-generated portions when registering a work and disclaim them; what remains protected is the human contribution around the AI output. In practice, that means a raw Suno generation is not something you can register or defend as yours, even on a plan that grants "commercial rights." The license lets you use it; it does not make it your intellectual property.

The practical workaround is the same one studios use. Run your generated track through [Suno's built-in Studio DAW](/reviews/suno) and re-record the vocal yourself. Export MIDI from [Mureka](/reviews/mureka) or [AIVA](/reviews/aiva) and rebuild the arrangement in a real DAW. Layer live guitar over an [AI bassline from Soundful](/reviews/soundful). Every meaningful human layer moves more of the track into protectable territory, and independent creators have started documenting their edit chains for exactly this reason.

## The Label Settlements Rewrote the Rules

The second layer is the litigation history, because it explains why the tools work the way they do today.

Universal, Sony, and Warner sued Suno and Udio in 2024 for training on copyrighted recordings, roughly $500 million in claims. The settlements that followed reshaped both platforms. [Universal settled with Udio in October 2025](https://babl.ai/universal-music-settles-with-ai-startup-udio-paving-way-for-licensed-fan-made-music-platform/), and Udio disabled downloads entirely as part of the deal. Warner settled with both platforms in November 2025, and [as Billboard's analysis lays out](https://www.billboard.com/pro/what-suno-udio-licensing-deals-mean-future-ai-music/), Suno's deal keeps its service intact but changes what users get: free users can play and share only, paid users get metered download limits starting 2026, and Suno must train on licensed works going forward.

Sony has not settled. Its case against both platforms continues, fact discovery closed in late September 2026, and the core fair-use question is not expected to reach a definitive US ruling before 2027. The practical takeaway for creators: **the commercial rights you have today are stable enough to use, but the export rules around downloads can change again.** If your workflow depends on downloading tracks, check the current terms before building a catalog on one platform.

## Platform Rules Decide Where Your Music Can Live

Even with a valid license from your tool, distribution platforms add their own layer. Spotify and Apple Music have required AI disclosure through the DDEX standard since late 2025, and failing to declare AI involvement can get tracks demonetized or removed. Spotify has also aggressively removed spam and impersonation content, over 75 million tracks in the year ending September 2025.

Two rules of thumb cover most situations. First, **watermarks are not licenses**: [Suno added audio watermarks and upload screening](/reviews/suno) in 2026, which identifies provenance but does not clear a track or protect it from Content ID claims. Second, if a listener could mistake your track for a human artist's work, disclose it. YouTube's Content ID does not care that a tool licensed the output to you; a claim can still land on your video.

## How to Stay Safe

- **Check the tier.** Commercial rights are a paid-tier feature almost everywhere. Free-tier output from Suno, Mureka, and Soundful is personal use only.
- **Prefer tools with clean training data** if your releases face scrutiny. [Stable Audio](/reviews/stable-audio) (licensed AudioSparx library) and [Soundful](/reviews/soundful) (Fairly Trained certification) built their pitches around avoiding the litigation cloud.
- **Add human authorship to anything you register.** Original lyrics, re-recorded vocals, MIDI reconstruction, and arrangement edits are the documented paths to protectable work.
- **Keep export receipts.** Save the plan screenshot, the generation date, and your edit history. If a claim lands, that paper trail is your defense.
- **Watch the download rules.** Udio currently cannot export at all. Suno meters downloads on paid plans. Tools that let you rebuild in a DAW, like Mureka and AIVA with MIDI export, leave you less exposed to platform changes.

## FAQ

**Can I monetize a video that uses AI music?** Yes, if your tool tier grants commercial rights. Suno Pro at $10/month, Mureka Pro at $9/month, and every paid Stable Audio tier all permit monetized use. The video platform is the bigger variable: disclose AI use on Spotify and Apple, and expect YouTube's Content ID to behave independently of your tool's license.

**Do I own the copyright to AI-generated music?** Not for pure AI output. The US Copyright Office protects human authorship, and the Supreme Court's March 2026 decision in Thaler v. Perlmutter kept that rule in place. You own what you contribute: lyrics, vocals, edits, arrangement. AIVA's Pro plan grants the strongest contractual claim to the composition itself, but copyright registration still depends on human contribution.

**Why can I not download from Udio anymore?** Udio disabled downloads in late 2025 as part of its settlement with Universal Music Group. The platform is transitioning to a licensed model, and until that launches, you can generate music but cannot export it. Our [Udio review](/reviews/udio) tracks the status.

**Is AI music training data legal?** Undecided in the US. Sony's litigation against Suno and Udio continues, and a definitive fair-use ruling is not expected before 2027. In Europe, a Munich court ruled against Suno in a GEMA case in July 2026. Tools trained on licensed libraries, like Stable Audio and Soundful, sidestep the question entirely.

**Which AI music tool gives the most commercial rights?** Mureka Pro at $9/month has the cheapest full commercial rights. AIVA Pro at €49/month goes further with 100% copyright ownership and zero royalty sharing, the strongest grant in the category, though it only generates orchestral and cinematic music.
